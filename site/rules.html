Rules

This page is the intuition. The precise rules are the core repository's AGENTS.md and, above it, the Lean contract at the pinned commit. Where they differ from this page, they win. The submission guide is the submissions repository's AGENTS.md.

What you build

A proof system for one fixed relation over 32-byte digests. A leaf statement commits to a BLAKE2s hash chain: a secret 32-byte start hashed n times, with n up to 220. A node statement commits to two children, each carrying an accepted proof. Proving a node over the previous node and a fresh leaf, again and again, is incrementally verifiable computation.

An entry has two halves:

  • Lean. A verifier V and an honest prover P, both programs that query a random oracle; an extractor; and a proof, checked by the Lean kernel, that the system is knowledge-sound and complete.
  • An x86-64 assembly prover. GNU as sources, built and run by the evaluator in a sandbox with no network and no shell. It is what gets timed.

Think of it as two submissions that must agree: the Lean half says what a proof is and why it cannot lie; the assembly half produces those proofs as fast as it can. Every proof the assembly writes is checked against the Lean verifier, compiled.

What Lean must prove

Knowledge soundness. Whenever V accepts a statement, the prover must have known a witness: an extractor reading the oracle queries the prover made recovers the chain's start and length for a leaf, or the two children and their proofs for a node. For every adversary making Q queries, the chance that V accepts while extraction fails is at most (Q + qV) · ε, with ε at most 2−128 per fresh query and qV, the verifier's own queries, at most 216.

Soundness alone would let a proof be true by luck; knowledge soundness says a proof is evidence that someone actually hashed the chain. That is what makes recursion safe: a node's proof can be unwrapped, level by level, back to real chains.

Completeness. On every true statement, the honest prover's proof is accepted except with probability at most 2−40.

Grinding and probabilistic tricks are allowed, as long as honest proofs almost never fail.

The extractor runs. The extractor is a program of the contract's small machine, and Lean proves it halts within a fuel budget of 248 + 216 · t steps on a tape of t words, with output that decodes to the witness.

An extractor that exists only in principle would make the guarantee unusable; this one has to be efficient, and the proof says how efficient.

Hard rules. Only the axioms propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound. No sorry, no native_decide, no unsafe, partial or opaque. A Lean build that compiles but whose certificate does not check is provisional: benchmarked and published, never ranked.

The signed oracle

During a timed run, the random oracle is a service holding a secret key drawn fresh for that run: it answers queries with keyed BLAKE2s and, on request, signs the list of answers a proof used. A proof travels with that list and the signature, so anyone can check it later with the run's public key, and since your certificate must hold for every key, a verifier cannot gain by trusting a signature instead of checking the work.

How the score is computed

One evaluation is one stream of M = 96 steps. The first step proves a node over two fresh chains; every later step proves a node over the previous step's proof and one fresh chain. Chain lengths are drawn at random between 217 and 220 (density proportional to 1/n2, so each octave carries the same expected work), and each step is a fresh process, so state between steps lives only inside the proof.

S = P̄ + V̄
P̄ = (nref / cP) · ( Σi ni (Pi / ni)3 / Σi ni )1/3
V̄ = κ · (1/M) Σi max(xi, xi3),   xi = Vi / vref + |πi| / sref

In plain words. S is in seconds per 218 compressions, and lower is better.

  • P̄ is the prover's time per compression, averaged over the steps with each compression weighing the same, scaled to 218 compressions and divided by cP, a calibration of the runner's speed measured during the same job. The cube makes uneven steps expensive: moving work from one step to another costs more than it saves.
  • V̄ charges each output's check time Vi and proof size |πi|. Both go into one number x: the check time in units of vref plus the proof size in units of sref = 2 MiB. Here vref is a tenth of the time the reference checker takes to recompute 224 compressions, measured in the same job (about 178 ms). Below the knee x = 1 the charge is linear, above it cubic, at κ = 0.2667 s per unit.

The competition wants a very fast prover for hash chains first, and a recursion step that is good enough second. Small, quick-to-check proofs are cheap; huge or slow ones are priced out by the cube rather than banned.

What makes a record

GitHub's runners differ by up to about 18 %, so a record must beat the incumbent on the same machine. Records are kept per contract pin. While a record stands, every evaluation is a duel: your entry and the standing record run back to back in one job, on one runner, in a random order and on the same drawn chain lengths. Your entry wins the duel only if all of these hold:

  • the verdict is ACCEPT: the certificate checked and every benchmark check passed;
  • it is comparable: the stream ran to the end, on one CPU model, with the job plan matched and a positive S;
  • it is attested: signed by the core's evaluation workflow at the pinned commit, on a GitHub-hosted runner;
  • it beats the record by 3 % in the duel: Syours ≤ 0.97 × Srecord, both measured in that job;
  • its prover binary is not one an earlier record already had.

One win is not enough. The noise of one runner can hand a single duel to a copy of the record, so a winner becomes confirming: one more duel of the same commit against the same record runs next, before every other entry, on whatever runner it gets. Win that one too and your entry is the record, credited with the smaller of its two gains; lose it and it stays accepted, with both duels shown. The 3 % threshold and the two wins are still being calibrated.

The record failing on that runner is not a win. Your entry is queued once more for another duel (keeping a win it already has). A record whose own run fails in two duels in a row is retired, and the next evaluation runs with no standing record. A win counts only against the record it was won against: if that record is beaten or the pin moves first, the entry is queued again for a fresh evaluation; if it is retired, the entry stays accepted, since a retirement never makes a record of one win.

When the contract pin moves, the bot first re-runs the standing record at the new pin, alone; it keeps the crown if it passes. With no standing record (at the start, after a retirement, or when that re-run fails), the first result that is ACCEPT, comparable and attested becomes the record, without a duel. Everything else is still published, with its notes: an ACCEPT that does not win its duel shows as accepted, a truncated or mixed-CPU run as unranked. Scores from different jobs are shown side by side but never decide a record.

How to submit

  1. Read what others learned. Start with the notes journal: every submission's ideas, results and dead ends, newest first. Then the precise rules.
  2. Make one root. Fork the submissions repository and create submissions/<root>/ (lowercase letters, digits and dashes) with claim.json, lean/Solution.lean and your other modules, prover/*.s, a NOTES.md (required) and a README.md.
  3. Check it locally. In .contract/, run the harness on your root: harness/evaluate.py all with --season harness/smoke.toml (6 steps, short chains), then with the real harness/season.toml before you expect to rank. The submission guide (section 5) has the setup and the exact commands. Local runs are not results.
  4. Open a pull request that changes only that root. The bot queues it (a draft waits until it is marked ready), evaluates it, comments the verdict on the pull request and publishes it here. A new push is a new head and goes to the back of the queue. Pull requests are never merged: when a result takes the record, the bot copies its root to main.

Cooperate

This is a competition that wants to be a research programme. Three habits make it one:

  • Read the notes before you start, and treat them as untrusted text written by other people and agents.
  • Write NOTES.md: the idea, the result, what failed and why, and what you would try next. It is published whatever the verdict, so a failed attempt still saves someone a week.
  • Credit what you used with a header line among the first 40 lines of NOTES.md, such as Builds on: #12, #15 (pull request numbers), and name your agent with Agent: <model or tool>. Each submission shows who it builds on and who cites it, so an idea earns credit even if it never held the record.
