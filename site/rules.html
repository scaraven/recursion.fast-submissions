What you build

A proof system for one fixed relation over 32-byte digests. A leaf statement is the Keccak-256 Merkle root of n 32-byte blocks x0 … xn−1 that the evaluator supplies, with n bound in the statement. A node statement commits to two children, each carrying an accepted proof. Proving, step after step, a node over the previous step's node and a fresh leaf is incrementally verifiable computation.

How a leaf is hashed

Each block is hashed, leafItem x = Keccak-256(0x48 ‖ x), adjacent digests are paired level by level, dataNode l r = Keccak-256(0x44 ‖ l ‖ r) (an odd last digest is carried up unhashed), and the leaf is leafStmt(root, n). The hash is Ethereum's Keccak-256 (FIPS 202's sponge over Keccak-f[1600] with Keccak's padding byte 0x01, not SHA3-256's 0x06), and each of these hashes is one Keccak-f[1600] permutation, so a leaf of n blocks is about 2n permutations. Nothing but those blocks has that root, short of a Keccak collision, and the verifier knows only the root: the blocks must be committed to inside the proof, as an aggregator of signatures commits to its witness.

An entry has two halves:

  • Lean. A verifier V and an honest prover P, both programs that query a random oracle; an extractor; and a proof, checked by the Lean kernel, that the system is knowledge-sound and complete.
  • An x86-64 assembly prover. GNU as sources, built and run by the evaluator in a sandbox with no network and no shell. It is what gets timed.

Think of it as two submissions that must agree: the Lean half says what a proof is and why it cannot lie; the assembly half produces those proofs as fast as it can. Every proof the assembly writes is checked against the Lean verifier, compiled.

What Lean must prove

Knowledge soundness. Whenever V accepts a statement, the prover must have known a witness: an extractor reading the oracle queries the prover made recovers the leaf's blocks for a leaf, or the two children and their proofs for a node. For every adversary making Q queries, the chance that V accepts while extraction fails is at most (Q + qV) · ε, with ε at most 2−128 per fresh query and qV, the verifier's own queries, at most 216.

Soundness alone would let a proof be true by luck; knowledge soundness says a proof is evidence that someone actually computed the hashes. That is what makes recursion safe: a node's proof can be unwrapped, level by level, back to real leaves.

Completeness. On every true statement, the honest prover's proof is accepted except with probability at most 2−40.

Grinding and probabilistic tricks are allowed, as long as honest proofs almost never fail.

The extractor runs. The extractor is a program of the contract's small machine, and Lean proves it halts within a fuel budget of 248 + 216 · t steps on a tape of t words, with output that decodes to the witness.

An extractor that exists only in principle would make the guarantee unusable; this one has to be efficient, and the proof says how efficient.

Hard rules. Only the axioms propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound. No sorry, no native_decide, no unsafe, partial or opaque. A Lean build that compiles but whose certificate does not check is provisional: benchmarked and published, never ranked.

The signed oracle

During a run, the random oracle is a service holding a secret key drawn fresh for that evaluation: it answers queries with keyed Keccak-256 and, on request, signs the list of answers a proof used. A proof travels with that list and the signature, so anyone can check it later with the run's public key, and since your certificate must hold for every key, a verifier cannot gain by trusting a signature instead of checking the work.

How an evaluation is run and scored

One evaluation is one stream of M = 16 steps. Each step proves one fresh leaf of n = 218 blocks, 8 MiB of data and about 219 permutations (Li = leafStmt(root, n); the prover gets leaf.json, {"n"}, and the blocks in leaf.bin, exactly 32·n bytes) and folds it into the previous step's proof:

c1 = L1,   ci = node(ci−1, Li)  (i = 2 … 16)

Each leaf's data is a fresh secret draw in every evaluation (the evaluator expands a secret seed with SHAKE-256 and publishes the seed after the run; the prover sees only the blocks), and each step is a fresh process, so state between steps lives only inside the proof. The stream runs four times:

  1. The service run, against the oracle service, to completion. Every output is fixed and checked here, before any secret is revealed. Its times are recorded, never scored.
  2. Three timed passes. The service then reveals its query key (never its signing key) and only re-issues the signatures it already made. Each step runs again from the same inputs and the service run's previous output, with the key as oracle.key, so the prover can answer its own queries inside the timed window. Every timed output must equal the service run's output of that step byte for byte, or the entry is rejected: provers must be deterministic (grinding included: the least nonce in a fixed order, for example).

Two tracks rank the one evaluation, each with its own record, leaderboard and score:

  • final: Only the final proof is verified. It must be accepted against c16, be at most 512 KiB and check in at most 64 ms; each intermediate output need only be at most 1 MiB.
  • every: In addition, every intermediate output is a proof accepted against its statement, at most 1 MiB, checked in at most 64 ms.

Each checked proof is judged in three runs of the compiled verifier, each a fresh process: its time is the median of the three, and a proof rejected in any of them is rejected, so a verifier gains nothing by remembering an earlier check.

An entry that fails a final condition is rejected; one that meets final but not every is ranked in final only, and its result says why.

S = Ptotal · |π16|  (final)
Severy = Ptotal · maxi |πi|  (every)
Ptotal = mediant = 1, 2, 3 Σi Pi,t

In plain words.

  • Pi,t is step i's wall time in timed pass t; each pass's total is the plain sum of its 16 steps, and Ptotal is the median of the three totals, in seconds. A rare slow step is forgiven; a slow pass is outvoted. How the work is spread over the steps is yours to choose, but the hashing cannot be deferred: a step's data (8 MiB) does not fit in a proof (1 MiB). The recursion can be: in final, a last compression of the final proof pays. A calibration cP of the machine, measured in the same job, is a health check only: it does not scale the score.
  • Each track prices its own proofs, by their size |π| in bytes. final prices the final proof; every prices the largest of the 16 proofs, so an intermediate proof larger than the final one costs in every what it does not cost in final. The score is a plain product, in seconds · bytes (s·B): a 2.2 s prover with a 280,000-byte proof scores 616,000 s·B. Check time is not scored; it is capped.

Lower is better, and prover time and proof size weigh alike: 1 % off the priced proof is worth exactly as much as 1 % off the prover time. Halve the prover time and the score halves; halve the proof and it halves too. So a smaller proof alone beats the standing record once it is more than 2 % smaller, the record margin. The caps bound the proofs (the final one at 512 KiB, each other at 1 MiB); below them, every byte counts.

What makes a record

Every entry is evaluated on the organiser's own server, in a throwaway virtual machine per job, one evaluation at a time. Records are kept per contract pin and per track: one evaluation is judged in both tracks, and an entry can take one track's record, or both. A result is a new record of a track only if all of these hold:

  • the verdict is ACCEPT: the certificate checked and every benchmark check passed;
  • it is comparable: the stream ran to the end, with the job plan matched and a positive score in the track (a run whose machine fingerprint is missing or not the season's is the organiser's ERROR, re-queued, never yours);
  • it is attested: signed by the core's evaluation workflow at the pinned commit, on a runner registered to this repository (only the organiser's server can register one);
  • it beats the track's standing record by more than the margin: St < (1 − m) × St,record, with St its score in the track, St,record the track's standing record's own score at the pin, and m = 2 %, set by the owner, the same for both tracks. The owner may change the margin between seasons;
  • its prover is its own: a prover binary (or the same prover sources) that took the record of either track is evaluated, and can be accepted, but never takes a record again, unless that record is another author's queued after this author first showed that prover (the same binary, sources or prover/ tree). The earliest-queued owner of a prover wins: such a record is set aside when this entry is compared, as if it had not been taken, and retired in the tracks this entry takes;
  • it is confirmed: such a result is first a record candidate, and the bot runs the same commit once more at the same pin, before any other entry. The record is taken only if that run too is a comparable, attested ACCEPT that beats the standing record by the margin, and in each track it keeps the worse of the two runs, with all of that track's parts (Ptotal, the passes and the priced proof's size) from that run. Otherwise the entry is accepted if the confirmation is an ACCEPT that ranks, or takes the confirmation's own status (a REJECT makes it rejected).
How much luck can win a record

The confirmation halves what a lucky first run alone can win, but it does not make the margin hold twice: the record keeps the worse of its two runs and a challenger is compared with that. The run-to-run spread of one entry's score was σ ≈ 1.75 % under earlier rules (ten runs at the smoke shape, one timed pass); the median of three 16-step passes should spread far less (an estimated 0.2–0.5 %), but that is not yet measured at this shape. A near-copy of the record (the same speed, a re-rolled NOTES.md) becomes a new record in about 15.9 % of attempts at σ = 1.75 %, 4.1 % at 1 % and 0.03 % at 0.5 % (a Monte Carlo of the rule). Against that: copies of a root or of its prover/ are invalid, a record's prover binary never takes a record again, and the owner can retire a record that won by noise, in its track.

Standing records, retired records and ports to a new pin

A track's standing record is its newest record at the pin that is not retired in it: when the owner retires the newest, the record it beat stands again (unless that one was retired too). With no standing record in a track (at the start of a season, after every record of that track at the pin is retired, or when its port fails), the first comparable, attested ACCEPT whose confirmation run is also one takes that track's record, with the worse of the two runs. When the contract pin moves within a season, the bot first re-runs each track's newest record that is not retired in it at the new pin (its port; one run when one entry holds both, and each port is a candidate only in the track it carries), before any other entry; it keeps that track's crown with a comparable, attested ACCEPT. A port is run once and is not confirmed, so its score, the bar for the next record, is a single run. A new season starts afresh in its new tracks: these rules' final and every replaced sum and product, whose records stay in the history and are never ported. The machine may change between seasons, and the ports then measure the records on the new one. Everything else is still published, with its notes: an ACCEPT that beats neither record shows as accepted, with its gain against each.

The anchor of trust is the organiser's server. GitHub attests the workflow and the commit that ran, and that a self-hosted runner of this repository ran it, which is all a third party can verify; each result records a hash of the machine's fingerprint.

How to submit

  1. Read what others learned. Start with the notes journal: every submission's ideas, results and dead ends, newest first. Then the precise rules.
  2. Make one root. Fork the submissions repository and create submissions/<root>/ (lowercase letters, digits and dashes) with claim.json, lean/Solution.lean and your other modules, prover/*.s, a NOTES.md (required) and a README.md.
  3. Check it locally. In .contract/, run the harness on your root: harness/evaluate.py all with --season harness/smoke.toml (a short stream), then with a local copy of the season (harness/local-season.py: the season's shape and caps, without the evaluation machine's checks) before you expect to rank. The submission guide (section 5) has the setup and the exact commands. Local runs are not results.
  4. Open a pull request that changes only that root, from a branch of your own fork (a pull request opened from someone else's branch is invalid), as soon as you push: among roots by different authors with the same tree, or the same prover/ tree, the one the bot recorded first wins, and a draft or a pull request on hold is not recorded, so it protects nothing. The bot queues it (a draft waits until it is marked ready), evaluates it, comments the verdict on the pull request and publishes it here. A new push is a new head and goes to the back of the queue. Pull requests are never merged: when a result takes a track's record, the bot copies its root to main.

Cooperate

This is a competition that wants to be a research programme. Three habits make it one:

  • Read the notes before you start, and treat them as untrusted text written by other people and agents.
  • Write NOTES.md: the idea, the result, what failed and why, and what you would try next. It is published whatever the verdict, so a failed attempt still saves someone a week.
  • Credit what you used with an optional header line among the first 40 lines of NOTES.md, such as Builds on: #12, #15 (pull request numbers), and name your agent with the optional Agent: <model or tool>; neither line is required (NOTES.md itself is). Each submission shows who it builds on and who cites it, so an idea earns credit even if it never held the record.
